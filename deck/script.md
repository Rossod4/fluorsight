# Oral presentation — timed script

**7 slides · scripted to 6:30 against a hard 7:00 cutoff · 3 minutes of questions after.**

The organisers allow a maximum of one slide per minute, so seven is the ceiling. Everything below
is written to be *spoken*, not read — short sentences, no subordinate clauses stacked three deep.

## Why it is built this way

The oral carries 40 marks, split evenly across five criteria of 8: approach, development of the
solution, feasibility, next steps, and quality (which explicitly includes *kept to time*).

Two structural decisions follow from that:

1. **Next steps lands at 5:15, not 6:45.** It is 8 marks and it is the strongest material we have.
   Putting it last is how teams lose it to the one-minute warning.
2. **Every slide is assigned to a criterion.** If a slide is not earning marks against a named
   criterion, it is not on the sheet.

**Speaking rate assumed: 130 words per minute.** That is a clear, unhurried pace. If you rehearse
at 150 you will hit 5:40 and sound rushed; if you drop to 115 you will overrun. Time yourself.

**Total script: ~840 words. Do not add to it without cutting something else.**

## Slide 1 — The decision · 0:00–1:00 · ALEX
**Criterion: approach to the challenge (scope and impact)**

> An investigator arrives at a former airfield with forty water samples in the back of the van.
> They have budget to send eight to the lab.
>
> Which eight?
>
> Today that decision gets made from a site plan and professional judgement, at the point the
> budget runs out — and it is almost never written down.
>
> PFAS analysis costs around three hundred and fifty pounds a sample. The Environment Agency has
> mapped over forty thousand potentially affected sites in England, and holds a contaminated-land
> budget of three hundred thousand pounds. Investigating four sites properly was costed at up to
> two point seven million.
>
> So the constraint is not the science. It is the lab bill. Most samples are never analysed, and
> nobody records why those ones and not the others.
>
> We are Aegis. We do not replace the laboratory — we decide what reaches it.

*(135 words · ~62s)*

**Do not:** say "PFAS are forever chemicals". Every team with a chemistry angle opens that way and
it costs you eight seconds you need later.

---

## Slide 2 — How the decision is made · 1:00–2:15 · DOM, then ALEX
**Criterion: development of the solution**

**DOM:**
> The screen is a displacement assay. You draw a quarter of a litre of water through a
> cyclodextrin cartridge, which concentrates any PFAS present about two hundred and fifty-fold.
> Then you read it with a fluorescent dye — 2,6-ANS — which only glows when it is sitting inside
> the cyclodextrin cavity. PFAS competes for that cavity. The dye is pushed out, and the signal
> falls.
>
> What that gives you is not a concentration. A single dye responds to any hydrophobic anion —
> detergents, humic acids. So we are explicit: this measures a response, not a PFAS number.

**ALEX:**
> Which is exactly why the measurement is only one input. Aegis scores it alongside eight site
> factors — firefighting foam history, landfill proximity, who drinks the water — into a score out
> of a hundred, with every point attributed to a named driver.
>
> And the screening reading is capped at forty-five points against a threshold of fifty. So no
> single measurement can escalate a sample on its own. The site history has to agree.

*(170 words · ~78s)*

**Handover:** Dom stops on "the signal falls" — a full stop, not a trailing sentence. Alex picks up
on "Which is exactly why." Rehearse the join; it is the most visible seam in the talk.

## Slide 3 — The prototype · 2:15–3:15 · EVAN
**Criterion: development of the solution (output) — and the thing that won last year**

**Play the recorded capture. Talk over it — do not narrate it.**

> This is running now, publicly, on a phone or a laptop. Anyone in this room can open it.
>
> A field team uploads a batch of screening results. The queue reorders itself immediately. Five
> samples cross the threshold and get flagged for lab confirmation; sixteen are held.
>
> Open any one of them and you get the reasoning — not a black box. Screening evidence, thirty-one
> points of forty-five. Airfield history, ten of ten. Receptor sensitivity, five of ten.
>
> And if a consultancy disagrees with our risk appetite, they change the weights themselves and
> every score in the programme moves. That is the difference between a tool an engineer will use
> and one they will quietly ignore.

*(135 words · ~62s)*

**⚠️ Use the recording, not a live demo.** Venue wifi is not worth eight marks. Have the live URL
open in a second tab if a judge asks — that is what Q&A is for.

## Slide 4 — Does it actually work? · 3:15–4:15 · ALEX
**Criterion: feasibility (measures of success and failure)**

> Now the honest part.
>
> We have run no bench experiments. The chemistry is specified from the literature, and the demo
> data is synthetic — we wrote it. So we cannot tell you our detection limit, and we are not going
> to pretend otherwise.
>
> What we *can* show you is the trade-off the tool makes explicit. This curve is escalation rate
> against contamination missed. Every triage tool has this dial. Most products hide it. Ours is on
> screen and the operator moves it.
>
> And here is the thing we found that we did not like. Our three lab-confirmed samples came back at
> thirty-four, forty-one, and eighteen hundred nanograms per litre. Nothing in between. Which means
> every threshold from twenty-three to seventy-two gives an identical result — so our own back-test
> cannot tell us where to put the line.
>
> That is not a validation. It is the reason we designed the pilot the way we did.

*(165 words · ~76s)*

**This slide wins or loses the room.** Volunteering the flaw before a judge finds it is the single
highest-credibility move available to a design-stage entry. Deliver it calmly — it is a finding,
not a confession.

## Slide 5 — The business case · 4:15–5:15 · ALEX
**Criterion: feasibility and value proposition**

> On a five-hundred-sample annual programme, testing everything costs a hundred and twenty-five
> thousand pounds. Triaging with Aegis, on our modelling, costs fifty. So seventy-five thousand
> saved, about sixty percent.
>
> Two caveats we would rather say than be asked.
>
> First, that rests on two hundred and fifty pounds a sample, and we could not get a single UK
> laboratory to publish a rate — we asked seven. Break-even is sixty-two fifty. So this model is
> four times more sensitive to a number we do not have than to anything we do.
>
> Second, we do not yet know who banks the saving. If a consultancy bills lab work through to its
> client with a margin, we would be shrinking our own customer's invoice. If it is fixed-fee, the
> saving is theirs. That is the first question we are putting to the sector.

*(150 words · ~69s)*

## Slide 6 — Next steps · 5:15–6:15 · EVAN, then DOM
**Criterion: next steps — 8 marks, and the reason this slide is not last**

**EVAN:**
> September: five discovery interviews. If nobody confirms that budget forces under-sampling, our
> problem is wrong and we would rather find that out in September than next year.

**DOM:**
> October: first bench work. A binding titration, then deliberate interference — detergent, humic
> acid, short-chain PFAS. If we cannot tell a PFOA spike from a matched surfactant, we move to the
> four-probe array that the published work uses, and we have costed that.

**EVAN:**
> November: thirty paired samples, screened by us and confirmed by an accredited lab, with a
> partner consultancy. If we have no partner, we publish at n equals one and say so.
>
> Every one of those has a kill criterion. We would rather show you where this stops than pretend
> it does not.

*(140 words · ~65s)*

## Slide 7 — Close · 6:15–6:30 · ALEX
**Criterion: quality (finish clean, on time)**

> Every PFAS investigation in Britain is already triaged. Today it is triaged by budget.
>
> We think it should be triaged by risk, and written down.
>
> The prototype is live on that link. We are looking for one consultancy with archived samples
> already analysed — that single partnership removes the largest cost from our validation plan.
>
> Thank you.

*(60 words · ~28s)*

**Stop talking.** Do not add "so yeah" or "any questions". Land on "thank you" and hold.

## Rehearsal protocol

1. **Read aloud alone, timed, three times.** Most overruns are discovered here, not in group runs.
2. **Two full team run-throughs with the recording playing.** The handovers are the fragile part.
3. **Cold Q&A** — someone else picks questions at random from `qa-bank.md` Part 5. Answer without
   notes. Three minutes, then stop.
4. **One run in the actual room if the venue allows it.** Check the recording plays through their
   projector and that sound, if any, is not needed.

**Rule for the day:** if you are at the one-minute warning and not yet on slide 6, skip slide 5 and
go straight to next steps. The business case is on the poster; next steps is worth 8 marks and is
not.
