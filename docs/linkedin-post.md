# LinkedIn post — driving responses to the interest form

**Written 17 August 2026.** Goal: responses to the Google Form. Secondary goal: a post that
reflects well on Alex professionally.

Two constraints shaped every line below.

**1. This is public, permanent, and a judge can find it.** The poster carries a QR code to
`fluorsight.co.uk` and states plainly that there is no bench data and no measured detection limit.
A LinkedIn post claiming more than the poster does is the single cheapest way to lose the
credibility the entry is built on. Nothing below claims a working detector.

**2. Honesty is the engagement strategy, not a tax on it.** "Here is what we can't prove yet" is
what makes a stranger reply. A polished student post claiming a solved problem gets likes from
friends; an open problem gets answers from professionals.

## Post A — the main one (recommended)

> A lab test for PFAS in drinking water costs roughly £250 a sample.
>
> I say "roughly" because we checked the published pricing of seven UK laboratories and not one of
> them publishes a rate. You have to ask.
>
> That's the problem three of us have spent the summer on. If you can't see the price, and the price
> is high, you don't test broadly — you test where you already suspect something. Which means the
> samples nobody suspected never get looked at.
>
> So we've built Fluorsight: not a replacement for the lab, but a way to decide which samples are
> worth sending to it. A cheap field screen, a transparent risk score, and an explicit
> recommendation — send this one, monitor that one, leave the rest.
>
> What we have: a working decision tool you can open right now, with the scoring model fully visible
> and editable rather than hidden in a black box.
>
> What we don't have: bench data. The chemistry is designed and not yet tested. We have no measured
> detection limit and we don't claim one.
>
> Which is why I'm asking rather than announcing.
>
> If you've ever had to decide whether a water sample goes to a lab — environmental consultants,
> contaminated land officers, lab staff, water quality teams — I'd really value 3 minutes of your
> view. The form is in the comments, and the most useful response is the one telling us we've got
> it wrong.
>
> Built with [tag Dom] and [tag Evan] at the University of Bristol.
>

## How to post it

- **Put the form link in the first comment, not the post body.** LinkedIn demotes posts with
  outbound links. Post, then comment within a minute or two.
- **Tag Dom and Evan by name.** Reach, and it is accurate — this is a three-person project.
- **Tuesday–Thursday, 8–10am UK.** Best window for a professional audience.
- **Reply to every single comment**, ideally with a question back. Replies are worth more than
  likes for reach, and a comment thread with a real consultant in it is itself evidence.
- **Ask your department, your mentor, and any Bristol society to reshare.** A student network alone
  will not reach contaminated-land professionals; one reshare by an academic might.
- **Log it.** Date posted, impressions, comments, form responses. `do-this-next.md` items 6, 9 and
  10 need exactly this, and "we posted publicly and got N responses" is a legitimate market-survey
  finding whatever N turns out to be — including a disappointing one.

## Do not add

- Any figure presented as measured — no detection limit, no accuracy, no sensitivity.
- The 60% cost saving, unless you write "modelled" next to it. It comes from the assumed £250, which
  is itself unverified. It reads as a result and it isn't one.
- "Revolutionary", "game-changing", "disrupting". The post's whole credibility is that it doesn't
  talk like that.
